Engaging with Video Game Satire

Published on September 04, 2026

I played a video game called Spec Ops: The Line a few years ago. It’s an old game, released way before I played it, but I’d always been interested because I’d heard that it was an adaptation of Joseph Conrad’s late 19th-century novella Heart of Darkness. This would seem to contrast, of course, with the game’s being a third-person modern-military cover shooter set in a Dubai warzone. Then again, any game about the horrors of colonialism and the plunging depths of the human psyche would be, to me, a rollicking good time.

It’s one of my favourite games, I think. Its story follows a Delta Force captain and his team and gives them impossible decisions as well as an overall terrible time. Since gaming is an interactive medium, though, the player is naturally given the reins. You, the player, must then make decisions that can have horrific consequences, and they really are quite horrific; one particular point in the game makes this abundantly clear. Essentially, you, the player, must either take an enormous and rather irresponsible risk that produces devastating consequences, or the story will not progress. In other words, you could choose not to do something terrible, but you would never then complete the game. If you want to complete the game, you have to do the terrible thing; there are no other options. Some would say that since the game forces you to do something terrible, you can’t be held responsible for that coercion. Others would say that the right thing to do is to perhaps just stop playing there; persisting at all is a choice, in the end. Still others would say that the terrible thing is, of course, not real, and the real-life satisfaction of completing the game — and indeed getting one’s money’s worth, having purchased the game — surely takes precedence. And perhaps even others might say that the terrible thing isn’t in fact a terrible thing at all, and one must make the best decision possible even with limited information, or risk even worse consequences. Besides, isn’t it cheating to meta-game and Google the outcome? You can’t do that in real life, after all.

I enjoyed this meta-layer of debate and decision making. Spec Ops: The Line wanted to pose a question to the player: to what extent are you responsible for what happens within the framework you exist in? This is the type of question that many ask every day: if I buy a certain disreputable company’s products, am I in some way accountable for disreputable things they do? If I participate in a system that takes advantage of others — though I don’t oppress those others myself — to what extent am I still culpable for those others’ unfortunate circumstances? These are not easy questions and putting them into a video game certainly invites rumination about these things. Plenty of people would say that, overall, rumination over difficult questions is important.

I started playing Helldivers 2 not long ago, and I found similar questions surfacing. The game is clear satire of imperialist and colonialist thinking. After all, it’s a blatant homage to a 90’s sci-fi movie called Starship Troopers, which discusses similar themes through the well-known critical lens of space armies fighting alien insects. Players team up in groups of up to four to fight not only giant bugs but robots and squidlike zombie people, wreaking havoc in the name of vague notions of progress like ‘spreading democracy’ and ‘promoting liberty.’ However, the game also inadvertently invites players to wreak havoc in the name of just having dumb fun, blowing things up, and ironically shouting about the aforementioned vague notions. I’m not a fan of online voice chat, so I remained quiet whenever joining a stranger’s lobby, but almost invariably I found my teammates shouting about how the insects were scum, or inventing fake slurs to refer to robots, or whatever.

I don’t think this is necessarily a sign of general moral decline, or really a sign of anything; it was very clear from the fake-manly-man-voices that this was all ironic behaviour. It was satire in itself, making fun of people who might say those things for real, or at the very least, the idea of such people. But, as mentioned before, video games are a participatory endeavour. There is a kind of ‘closeness to the action’ which video games offer that movies or books don’t necessarily — or at least, games directly invite a player to engage with them as a medium in a way that movies or books don’t, thereby inviting players to bring personal stakes into any themes discussed.

Not long ago, I came across an academic article that discussed satire, written by a British literature expert named David Palumbo. He highlights Jonathan Swift’s way of seeing satire as distinguished between ‘private’ and ‘public,’ whereby the former reads more like an in-joke between friends, and the latter is trying to make a statement. The background is this: famous 18th-century British writer Jonathan Swift wrote an essay called A Modest Proposal for Preventing the Children of Poor People from Being a Burthen to Their Parents or Country, and for Making Them Beneficial to the Publick. This was a satirical essay which proposed that poor Irish people could alleviate their poverty by selling their children as food to rich English people. It was a critique of many things, but especially English treatment of the Irish. Palumbo points out that as the work gained popularity, Swift’s later writing regarding how he felt about the essay seemed to be just a touch bitter. Swift had written the essay for a laugh with his friends, and the in-joke was empowering for a little while. But since the essay couldn’t itself be an actionable tool for positive change — not that this was Swift’s aim anyway — and even a few members of the English aristocracy were finding the joke somewhat funny too by misinterpreting it, the whole thing stopped being funny for Swift. His ‘private’ satire among his friend group went a bit sour because it was indeed motivated by good intentions. One of Palumbo’s main points is that engaging in satire was Swift’s way of addressing his own powerlessness, and while there was some empowerment in creating satire, it was ultimately unsatisfying; that satire didn’t change his powerlessness.

I certainly don’t think Helldivers 2 is trying to dig too deeply into what satire is, and any perceived insensitivity isn’t necessarily cause for any kind of alarm. Video games, like any art media, have been basically doing whatever for as long as they’ve been around — you could find video games about all kinds of distasteful stuff, intentional or not, satirical or earnest. And I don’t think the makers of Helldivers 2 were intending anything distasteful, only that there’s ample room for debate as to which camp their satire sits in. The curtain of satire comes, after all, in many deniers, private or public, but I don’t really know what kind of satire Helldivers 2 is trying to pull off, and I wish it were easier to tell because it’s interactive.

To me, therein lies a risk of encouraging revelry, not irony, in the subject of its satire. To some extent, if you do something ironically for long enough, you might find a certain appeal to it or discover a fondness for it after all. At the least, you might even become desensitised to it or mistake your enjoyment of the satire for enjoyment of the subject of that satire. I brought up Spec Ops: The Line in the beginning because the creators were being intentional with it. Even their multiplayer deathmatch mode involved some thought as to how they were presenting their satire. That approach is a far cry from the way that Grand Theft Auto likes to do its satire, which seems to me to be entirely ‘private’: laughing at a ridiculous outside group from within an inside group.

Helldivers 2 seems to me to be a ripe opportunity, seeing that it’s still active and current as of writing, for players to investigate all these things, especially if one plays in teams of online strangers. Never mind the Vox Engines hailing mighty volleys of ordnance on you or the Bile Spewer acid worming its way through your clothes and into your skin. Think instead about Jonathan Swift, and think about the 18th-century English aristocracy laughing at the idea that they would ever eat babies, turning over the idea instead to mock then-British-Prime-Minister Robert Walpole for being rather large.

Source: David M. Palumbo, “From ‘Laughing’ to ‘Rayl[ing]’ with a ‘Few Friends’: A Modest Proposal as Private Satire.”
